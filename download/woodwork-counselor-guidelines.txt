Woodwork Merit Badge Overview
Woodworking combines practical skills, careful planning, and creativity. As you explore this
merit badge, you will learn about wood as a material, become familiar with woodworking tools
and techniques, and use plans and measurements to turn ideas into finished projects.
You will also explore how timber becomes lumber, how different woods are suited to different
purposes, and how skilled woodworkers use their knowledge to create useful and lasting
objects.
Character Development:
The Woodwork merit badge fosters character development by giving Scouts the chance to
practice patience, responsibility, and attention to detail. It encourages you to work
carefully and safely, solve problems when things do not go as planned, and take pride in
doing a job well.
Designing and building projects also develops creativity and perseverance as you work
step by step from an idea to something you can use, share, or give to others.

What Is Woodwork?
Woodwork is the skill of making, shaping, joining, and finishing objects made from wood. It
can involve anything from building a simple box or useful household item to making furniture,


toys, decorative pieces, and other carefully crafted projects.
Good woodworking starts with understanding the material. Different woods have different
qualities, and the way wood is measured, cut, shaped, joined, and finished all affects the final
result.
You don’t need a large workshop or lots of equipment to get
started with woodwork. Basic hand tools such as a saw,
hammer, plane, and brace and bit can be used to build useful
projects while helping you learn how wood behaves.
Key Topics in Woodwork
These are some of the topics you will cover in the woodwork merit badge:
Wood and lumber: Learn where wood comes from, how timber becomes lumber, and how
different types of wood are selected for different uses.
Workshop safety: Understand common hazards and how safe working practices and
protective equipment help prevent injuries.
Tools and techniques: Learn how woodworking tools are used, cared for, stored, and kept
in good working condition.
Measuring and planning: Use accurate measurements and working drawings to plan
projects before construction begins.
Building projects: Develop practical woodworking skills by cutting, shaping, joining, and
assembling wood.


Design and craftsmanship: Create your own project and work toward an accurate, useful,
and well-made result.
Woodworking careers: Explore the skills, training, and opportunities involved in
professional woodworking and carpentry.

Woodworking Hazards


Hazards can come from the tools and equipment you use, the materials you work with, and
the way your work area is set up. Some of the most likely hazards include sharp cutting edges
and moving parts, unsecured wood, flying chips and sawdust, loud noise, electricity, fumes,
and distractions.
Thinking about these hazards involves four steps:
Anticipate: Think about what could go wrong before you begin.
Help prevent: Take steps to stop an incident from happening.
Mitigate: Reduce the chance of harm or make the possible consequences less serious.
Respond: Know what to do if something does go wrong.


Flying Debris, Dust, and Noise
Anticipate: Think about whether the task could produce flying wood chips, sawdust, or
loud noise.
Help prevent: Set up the work correctly and use the appropriate protective equipment
for the task. Wear appropriate dust protection including safety glasses, dust mask and
hearing protection for power tools.
Mitigate: Limit your exposure to dust and loud noise, and keep others clear of the
immediate work area.
Respond: Stop working if debris gets into your eyes or you experience breathing or
hearing problems and get help as needed.


Loose Clothing, Jewelry, or Long Hair
Anticipate: Check your clothing, jewelry, and hair before working around tools or
equipment with moving parts.
Help prevent: Remove loose jewelry, secure loose clothing, and tie back long hair.
Mitigate: Keep your clothing and body clear of moving parts while equipment is
operating.
Respond: Stop the equipment if it is safe to do so and get help immediately if anything
becomes caught.


Fumes and Ventilation
Anticipate: Consider whether glues, adhesives, paints, varnishes, or other products could
give off harmful fumes.
Help prevent: Work in a properly ventilated area and follow the instructions for the
product you are using.
Mitigate: Use appropriate respiratory protection when needed and reduce your exposure
to fumes. N95 dust masks do not remove fumes and vapors. Use of an approved activated
carbon cartridge mask for work involving vapors and fumes.
Respond: Stop working and move away from the source if you feel unwell. Tell a
responsible adult and get further help if needed.


Unsecured Wood or Loss of Control
Anticipate: Think about how the wood needs to be held and supported before you begin
cutting, drilling, or shaping it. Check that your tools are sharp. Dull tools require more
force to use, which makes them harder to control and more likely to slip.
Help prevent: Secure the wood with clamps or a vise as needed, and support boards
properly when sawing.
Mitigate: Keep a firm grip on handheld tools and stop if the wood becomes loose or
unstable.
Respond: Put the tool down or switch it off safely before securing the workpiece again.


Sharp Tools and Moving Parts
Anticipate: Identify where blades, bits, cutting edges, moving parts, and your hands will
be while you work.
Help prevent: Keep your hands clear of cutting edges and moving parts, and work away
from your body.
Mitigate: Keep guards in place and stop if you begin to lose control of the tool or
workpiece.
Respond: Stop work, make the area safe, and get appropriate help if an injury occurs.


Electricity
Anticipate: Look for wet conditions, damaged equipment, and power cords that could
interfere with the work.
Help prevent: Never use a power tool in a wet or damp area. Use properly grounded
equipment. Ensure a suitable adult is supervising the use of electric tools in accordance
with Scouting policy.
Mitigate: Keep power cords away from blades and other moving parts.
Respond: Stop work and disconnect the power if it is safe to do so. Get adult or
emergency help when needed.


Clutter, Distractions, and Tiredness
Anticipate: Check that the work area is clear and that you are ready to concentrate before
you begin.
Help prevent: Keep your workspace orderly and put tools away safely when they are not
being used.
Mitigate: Stop working if you become distracted, tired, or sleepy.
Respond: Put the tool down or switch it off safely before dealing with a distraction or
taking a break.
Using Tools Safely


Safe woodworking also means choosing, checking, and using each tool correctly.
Use the right tool for the job and learn how to use it correctly before you begin.
Check your tools before use. Keep them clean, sharp, well maintained, and in good
working condition. Most cutting tools are designed to be sharp. Use of dull cutting tools
can be more dangerous due to excessive effort needed to make them work as designed.
Follow the manufacturer’s instructions for the tool you are using.
Keep safety guards in place.
Disconnect power tools before making adjustments or changing parts.
Use only tools that Scouts your age are permitted to use and follow Scouting America’s
requirements for adult supervision.
Safety Reminder
Scouts under 18 may not operate hazardous powered woodworking equipment
such as band saws, drill presses, table saws, or circular saws. When Scouts under
18 use permitted power tools, they must be supervised by a mature adult who is
experienced in using the tool.

First Aid – Minor Injuries


Even when you work carefully, injuries can happen. Keep a first-aid kit nearby and know how
to respond to common minor woodworking injuries.
Splinters
1. Remove the splinter with tweezers.
2. Wash the area with soap and water.
3. Apply antiseptic.
4. Cover the wound with an adhesive bandage.
Scratches and Minor Cuts
1. Wash the wound with soap and water.
2. Apply antiseptic.
3. Cover it with an adhesive bandage to help keep it clean.
First Aid – Serious Injuries
Even though they are rare occurrences, it is important to understand what to do if there is a
serious injury.


Severe Bleeding
Severe bleeding can quickly become life-threatening.
1. Stay safe: Make sure the scene is safe. Avoid contact with blood if possible; use protective
wear (e.g., gloves, eyewear/face shield, or another barrier if available.
2. Get help: Tell someone to call 911 and get an adult and a first-aid kit.
3. Stop the bleeding: Put gauze, a clean cloth, or clothing directly on the wound
Press hard and continuously
Keep pressing — don't keep lifting the dressing to check the wound
If blood soaks through, keep pressure on the wound and add more material on top
4. Assess if you should use a tourniquet: If it is life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg
and you have been trained to use a tourniquet, use a manufactured tourniquet. Follow its
instructions. Do not loosen or remove it after it is applied.
5. Stay with the person: Keep the person as comfortable and appropriately warm as possible.
Watch for changes in breathing or responsiveness and follow the directions of the 911
dispatcher.
6. Don't do more than you know how to do: If you have not been trained in wound packing or
other advanced bleeding-control techniques, don't improvise them. Keep pressure on the
bleeding and follow the emergency dispatcher's instructions.
Shock
Shock can occur after severe bleeding or another serious injury and is a medical emergency.
Signs can include pale, cool, or clammy skin, rapid breathing, a rapid weak pulse, restlessness
or confusion, dizziness, and thirst.
Treat the injury causing the shock, such as controlling severe bleeding.
Call or send someone for medical help.
Help the person lie down and monitor their breathing.
Keep the person warm with a blanket, coat, or other covering.

Protect Your Eyes, Hearing, and Lungs


Woodworking can expose your eyes and ears to hazards and create dust that you could
breathe into your lungs. Using the right protective equipment can help prevent lasting
damage.
Protect Your Eyes
Wear safety glasses when:
Hammering
Chiseling
Sanding
Using power tools (with appropriate supervision)
Safety glasses help protect your eyes from wood chips, dust, and other flying material. Safety
glasses should have an ANSI Z87 impact rating.
Protect Your Hearing
Wear hearing protection when using power tools. Many power tools produce enough noise to
damage your hearing. Noisy environments above 85 decibels requires hearing protection with
a 20 - 30 decibel noise reduction. For comparison a normal conversation is 60 decibels.


Wear a Dust Mask
A dust mask helps prevent you from breathing sawdust and other wood particles into your
lungs. Dust masks with an N95 rating are best.
Wear a dust mask whenever sanding, sawing, or doing other work that produces wood dust.
A dust mask protects against particles such as sawdust. When working
indoors with glues, adhesives, paints, varnishes, or other products that
can give off fumes, use proper ventilation and the appropriate
respiratory protection for the product.

All About Wood


Every piece of wood used in a woodworking project was once part of a living tree. Trees use
water and minerals from the soil, carbon dioxide from the air, and energy from sunlight to
grow and produce wood.
Wood is a versatile material that can be used to make everything from buildings and furniture
to toys, artwork, and musical instruments. Different woods have different qualities, and
understanding where wood comes from and how it behaves can help you choose the right
material for a project.
Getting to Know a Piece of Wood
Grain: The pattern of lines, curves, and shading created as the tree grows. Grain usually
runs lengthwise along a board.


Face: The wide surface of a board.
Edge: The narrow surface of a board.
End grain: The grain visible at either end of a board.
Knot: A hard area in the wood where a branch grew from the tree. Knots can change the
wood’s appearance and may affect how easy it is to work.

Timber and Lumber


The process of creating wood for woodworking involves several stages, from growing and
harvesting trees to milling, drying, grading, and sizing the boards.
What Is Timber?
Standing trees and their wood are called timber.
What Is Lumber?
Once timber has been cut and prepared for use, it becomes lumber.


Watch the Process for Trees Being Made into Lumber
Growing Timber


Foresters grow trees as a crop, although trees take much longer to mature than most farm
crops.
Trees can be grown from seeds or young trees called seedlings.
Seedlings may be raised in a nursery for one to four years before being planted in a
forest.
Depending on the type of tree and how it will be used, trees may grow for five to 30 years
or longer before they are ready to harvest.
Harvesting Timber


When trees are ready to harvest, foresters decide which trees should be cut and how the
forest will be managed afterward. Four common harvesting methods are described below.

Milling Timber


After selected trees are cut down:
1. The branches are removed and the trunks are cut into manageable logs.
2. The logs are transported to a sawmill.
3. At the mill, the bark is removed.
4. Saws cut the logs into boards.
5. The new boards, called green lumber, are edged and trimmed to straighten the sides and
make standard lengths.
Curing and Seasoning Lumber


To cure or season lumber means to dry it. Lumber needs to be dried because wood that
contains too much moisture can warp.
There are two main methods:
Air drying: Lumber is stacked outdoors so that sun and moving air gradually dry it.
Kiln drying: Lumber is placed in a kiln where temperature, moisture, and air movement
are controlled. This dries the wood more quickly and consistently than air drying.
Grading Lumber
Lumber is graded to describe its quality, appearance, or strength. Knowing the grade can help
you choose lumber that is suitable for your project.

Sizing Lumber
Lumber is sold in standard lengths, widths, and thicknesses. The size used to name a piece of
lumber is called its nominal size.
Lumber is measured before it is dried and surfaced smooth. Because drying causes wood to
shrink and surfacing removes more material, its finished dimensions are smaller than its
nominal dimensions.
For example, a board called a 2 × 4 is not actually 2 inches thick and 4 inches wide. Its finished
dimensions are usually about 1½ inches × 3½ inches.
Calculating Board Feet
Here’s how to calculate the board feet in an
8-foot 2 × 4 using its nominal dimensions (the
labeled size of the board before it is planed
and surfaced).
Formula:
Thickness × Width ÷ 12 × Length = Board Feet
Example:
2 × 4 = 8
8 ÷ 12 = 0.67
0.67 × 8 = 5.33 board feet
So, an 8-foot 2 × 4 contains about 5.33 board
feet.
Did You Know?
Woodworkers may also measure lumber using
a unit called a board foot. A board foot
measures the volume of wood rather than
simply its length.

Tool and Equipment Care


Using the right tool for the job makes woodworking easier, safer, and faster. Learning to use
tools correctly and taking good care of them also helps them work better and last longer.
Good tool care helps tools work properly and last longer.
Keep tools clean.
Keep tools properly lubricated where appropriate.
Protect metal surfaces from corrosion. A light film of rust-inhibiting oil can help protect
suitable metal tools.
Keep cutting tools sharp and in good condition.
Check tools before use and do not use damaged equipment.
Follow the manufacturer’s care and maintenance instructions for equipment when
applicable.
As you review the tools below, think about any care they might need.
Measuring and Checking Tools


Tape Measure (Shown Below) or Rule
Use a rule or tape measure to measure wood accurately.
How to use it:
Place the rule so its measurement marks are close against the wood.
Use a soft-lead pencil to mark the measurement.
Make a short dash in the direction of the cutting line rather than marking the point
with a dot.


Try Square
A try square helps you mark and check 90-degree angles and make sure an edge or end is
square.
How to use it:
Hold the thick handle firmly against the edge of the board.
Let the blade lie across the face or end of the board.
Check whether the end of the board lines up exactly with the blade.
To mark a square cutting line, hold the handle against the edge and draw along the
blade.


Carpenter’s Square
A carpenter's square, or framing square, is a larger square.
How to use it:
Use it to mark or check right angles.
It is particularly useful for boards that are too wide for a try square.


Combination Square
A combination square has a movable head that can be locked in position along its rule.
How to use it:
Use it like a try square to mark or check a 90-degree angle.
Use its 45-degree setting when you need to mark a 45-degree angle.
A combination square is also useful to mark boards at a specific measurement
repeatably.


Level
A level checks whether a board, shelf, or other workpiece is level or plumb.
How to use them:
Use a level to check whether something is horizontal, or level.
Use it to check whether something is vertical, or plumb.
A plumb line is a different option to check vertical alignment. When its weighted
string hangs freely, it forms a vertical line.
Fastening and Holding Tools


Hammer (Claw Hammer Shown Below) and Mallet
Both tools are used for striking, but they have different jobs. Wear safety glasses when
using a hammer and nail set.
Claw hammer: Use it to drive and remove nails.
How to drive a nail:
Hold the nail by the shank, well below the head.
Tap it lightly two or three times until it stands by itself.
Move your hand clear.
Swing from the elbow and strike the nail head squarely.
Stop striking when the nail head is flush with the wood. Do not strike the wood after
the nail is flush.
How to remove a nail:
Slip the claw under the nail head.
If needed, place a piece of scrap wood beneath the hammer head to protect your
project.
Keep your other hand clear of the hammer and nail as you remove the nail.
Pull back on the handle to draw out the nail.
Mallet: A mallet usually has a wooden or rubber head. Use it for striking wood or
woodworking tools, such as tapping a chisel. Do not use it to drive nails.


Nail Set
A nail set is used to drive a nail slightly below the surface of the wood. It is often used
with finishing nails when a project will be stained or varnished.
How to use it:
Drive the finishing nail as close to the wood surface as possible without denting the
wood.
Place the point of the nail set on the nail head.
Tap the nail set to drive the nail head below the surface.
Fill the small hole with wood filler when appropriate.


Screwdrivers: Standard/Slotted (Top Image) and Phillips (Bottom Image)
Two common types are the standard, or slotted, screwdriver and the Phillips screwdriver.
How to use them:
Match the screwdriver tip to the type of screw.
Choose the correct size.
Make sure the tip fits properly into the screw head.
Do not use a tip that is too wide, too narrow, or too thick. A poor fit can damage the
screw head or wood and may cause the screwdriver to slip.


Vises (Shown Below) and Clamps
Vises and clamps hold wood securely while you cut, shape, fasten, or glue it.
How to use them:
Secure the wood before cutting or otherwise working on it.
Use clamps to hold pieces together while joining them with nails, screws, or glue.
A vise attached to a workbench can also hold the work securely.
If you use a machinist’s vise, place scrap wood between the vise jaws and your project
to protect the wood.
Cutting and Shaping Tools


Handsaws (Japanese Saw Shown Below)
Different handsaws are designed for different kinds of cuts. Choosing the right saw can
make your work easier and more accurate.
Ripsaw: Use for cutting with the grain, lengthwise along a piece of wood.
Crosscut saw: Use for cutting across the grain, such as when cutting a board to
length.
Backsaw: Use for precise cuts and fine work, such as joinery. Its thin blade is
reinforced by a metal strip along the back.
Japanese saw: Unlike conventional handsaws, which cut on the push stroke, a
Japanese saw cuts on the pull stroke. Its thinner blade can provide good control with
less effort.
How to use them:
Secure the wood, keep the saw aligned with your cutting line, and use smooth, controlled
strokes without forcing the blade.


Coping Saw
A coping saw has a thin, replaceable blade that can be angled, making it useful for cutting
curves and interior shapes. How to make an interior cut:
Bore a hole at least 1/4 inch wide just inside the shape you want to cut.
Remove the blade from the coping saw frame.
Pass the blade through the drilled hole.
Reattach the blade to the frame.
Saw along the marked cutting line.


Rasp
A rasp has sharp teeth for removing wood and smoothing rough surfaces.
How to use it:
Push the rasp forward across the surface you want to shape or smooth.
Lift it away from the wood for the return stroke.
Repeat as needed. A rasp cuts as it moves forward.


Chisel
A chisel has a strong, flat blade with a beveled cutting edge. It is used to chip, shave, and
shape wood.
How to use it:
Remove small amounts of wood rather than cutting too deeply.
Start the blade slightly inside your guideline.
Push the chisel by hand when possible.
If you need more force, tap the handle with a wooden mallet.
Always direct the cutting edge away from your body and hands.


Plane
Planes are used to smooth and straighten rough or uneven surfaces or remove small
amounts of thickness or width.
Common types include:
Smoothing plane: For general smoothing.
Jack plane: For planing longer pieces of wood.
Block plane: For smoothing end grain and trimming smaller pieces.
How to use it:
Secure the wood with a vise or clamps.
Hold the plane with one hand on the handle and the other on the knob.
Push it firmly along the entire length of the wood without lifting it during the cutting
stroke.
Apply more pressure to the front of the plane as you begin the cut.
Apply more pressure to the back as you finish.
Plane with the grain. If the plane sticks, digs in, or lifts chips, turn the wood around
and try the opposite direction.
Allow only a small amount of the blade to project below the sole.
Use a sharp blade.
When you are not using the plane, rest it on its sole to help avoid damaging the blade.


Drilling Tools
Hand Drill (Shown Below) and Brace and Bit
Both tools are used to bore holes in wood by hand.
Hand drill: Uses cutting bits or twist drills for smaller holes, generally up to 1/4 inch.
Brace and bit: Uses larger auger bits to bore larger holes.
How to use them:
Keep the drill or bit straight as you bore the hole.
Apply pressure straight into the wood rather than sideways to avoid bending or
breaking the bit.
When using a brace and bit, you can help prevent splintering by drilling until the tip of
the bit just appears on the opposite side.
Turn the wood over and finish the hole from the other side.


Electric Drill
An electric drill can make drilling easier.
How to use it:
Secure the workpiece before drilling.
Make a small starting dent in the wood to help prevent the bit from skittering across
the surface.
Put a thick piece of scrap wood beneath the drilling area to protect your work surface
when the bit breaks through.
Let the drill do the work rather than forcing it with excessive pressure.
Keep the drill running as you finish the hole.
Reduce pressure as the bit approaches breakthrough and maintain control of the
drill.
Gently withdraw the bit while it is still turning to reduce the chance of it binding in
the hole.
Sanding Tools


Sanding Block
A sanding block helps keep sandpaper flat against the surface of the wood.
How to use it:
Wrap the sandpaper securely around the sanding block.
Sand with the grain of the wood.
Begin with a coarser grit when more material needs to be removed.
Move to finer sandpaper to produce a smoother finish.
If you don’t have a store-bought sanding block, make one from a block of wood about 6" x
31⁄2" x 1". Pad the top and bottom with cork or felt to protect the work. Wrap the sandpaper
around the block and hold it against the sides of the block as you work.


Orbital Sander
An orbital sander has a flat sanding pad that moves in short, rapid strokes.
How to use it:
Secure the workpiece before sanding.
Wear a dust mask.
Do not press down on the sander. Let the weight of the tool do the work.
Start the sander before placing it on the workpiece.
Keep it moving continuously over the surface.
Whenever possible, sand with the grain.
Begin with coarse sandpaper and finish with finer paper.
Keep the sander running as you lift it from the wood before switching it off. This helps
prevent gouges in the surface.
Tool and Equipment Storage


Proper tool storage is essential for tool maintenance and safety.
Store metal tools safely indoors rather than leaving them outside.
Give every tool a secure storage place.
Put tools away when you finish using them.
Do not leave tools lying around where they could be damaged or become a hazard.
Store tools so cutting edges and other easily damaged parts are protected.
A well-cared-for tool is easier and safer to use. Clean up your work area
and return each tool to its proper place at the end of every
woodworking session.

Tool Sharpening
Sharp cutting tools are easier to control and produce better results. A dull blade requires
more force and is more likely to slip, which can damage your work or cause an injury.


Safety Reminder
Secure the stone so it cannot move while you sharpen
Keep your fingers and hands away from the cutting edge while sharpening
Follow the sharpening stone manufacturer’s instructions, including the recommended
lubricant
Keep the cutting edge pointed away from your body
Use controlled strokes and do not force the chisel
Wear eye protection if the sharpening method or manufacturer’s instructions indicate a
risk of particles or splashing
Detailed Demonstration of Tool Sharpening
Most wood-cutting tools have two bevels at the cutting edge:
Ground bevel (also called primary bevel): The larger, main bevel of the blade.
Sharpening bevel (also called secondary bevel): The smaller bevel at the cutting edge.
This is the bevel you work on when sharpening.


Did You Know?
You should never throw cutting tools into a
toolbox. Rough handling can nick and dull
freshly sharpened edges.
Using Sharpening Stones
A sharpening stone, or whetstone, is used to restore a sharp cutting edge. Follow the
manufacturer's instructions for the sharpening stone, including the recommended lubricant.
Secure the stone so it cannot move while you sharpen. Keep your fingers and hands away
from the cutting edge while sharpening.


When needed, work through progressively finer stones:
1. Rough or coarse
2. Medium
3. Fine
4. Smooth or polishing
Finish by stropping the edge on a piece of leather. This removes the thin wire edge, or burr,
left by sharpening.

Sharpening a Plane
Remove the cutting iron, or blade, from the plane before sharpening it.
Example: 30-degree sharpening angle


How to sharpen it:
Lay the cutting iron facedown on the whetstone.
Slowly raise the blade until the sharpening bevel sits flat against the stone.
Keep the sharpening angle constant and parallel to the stone.
Move the blade across the stone with the cutting edge facing away from you.
Turn the blade over onto its flat side.
Keep the back of the blade flat against the stone and push it forward.
Finish by stropping the edge on leather to remove the burr.
Sharpening a Chisel
A chisel is sharpened using the same basic technique as a plane iron.


How to sharpen it:
Set the sharpening bevel against the stone at the correct angle.
Keep that angle constant as you push the chisel forward across the stone.
On the return stroke, pull the chisel back lightly without applying pressure.
Turn the chisel over and keep its flat side completely flat against the stone.
Stone both sides as needed.
Finish by stropping the cutting edge to remove the burr.
Once you find the correct sharpening angle, keep it constant. Rolling or
changing the angle as you sharpen can prevent you from producing an
even, sharp edge.

How To Create a Working Drawing
A working drawing is a detailed, accurate sketch that shows the size and shape of the project,
the dimensions of each piece, and how the pieces fit together. This example is for the to-do


list holder.
To make or use a working drawing:
Sketch the project: Show the overall shape and the main parts.
Add the views you need: A simple project may need only a front and top view or a front
and side view. More complex projects may need top, front, and side views.
Add dimensions: Mark the length, width, thickness, hole locations, and other
measurements needed to make each part.
Show how the parts fit together: Include enough detail to understand where pieces join
and how the project will be assembled.
Use the drawing as your guide: Refer back to it as you measure, mark, cut, drill, and
assemble each piece.
Your drawing does not need extra views if they do not add useful information. Include enough
detail to clearly show how to cut and assemble the project.
How To Square, Measure, and Cut Lumber


Before cutting the parts for a project, you need a straight, square edge and end to measure
from. This gives you a reliable starting point and helps the pieces fit together correctly.
1. Check and Square the Lumber
Check the end and edges of the board with a try square.
If an end is not square, mark a square line across the board.
Saw away the excess wood and use a plane if needed to bring the edge accurately to the
line.
Use the squared end as your starting point for later measurements.
2. Measure and Mark
Check the working drawing for the dimensions of the part you are making.
Measure from a squared end or edge.
Mark the measurement with a pencil.
Use a try square or straightedge to extend the mark into an accurate cutting line.
Measure twice before you cut. Accurate measuring and squaring at the beginning can
save a lot of time correcting mistakes later.
3. Cut and Plane
Wear appropriate eye protection when sawing, planing, or performing other
woodworking tasks that may produce chips or dust.


Secure the lumber before cutting.
Keep your hands clear of the cutting path and position the work so the tool cannot contact your
body if it slips.
Saw carefully along the marked cutting line.
When you need to plane an edge to an exact size, cut slightly outside the line so there is a
small amount of wood left to remove.
Plane down to the marked line.
Check the finished piece for size and square before moving on.
When accuracy matters, leave a little extra wood when you
saw and remove the final amount with a plane. It is much
easier to remove a small amount of wood than to replace
wood that has been cut away.

To make a beveled edge:
Mark a line showing the angle you want to create.

A rounded edge takes this process further. Plane away the sharp edge to create the rounded
shape, then sand it smooth with the grain.

(c) Miter, Dowel, or Mortise and Tenon joints


For this option, make a project that uses a miter, dowel, or mortise and tenon

Miter Joint
A miter joint joins two pieces at an angle. For a square 90-degree corner, each piece is cut
at 45 degrees. A miter box can help guide the saw accurately.
Miter joints are often used where appearance matters, such as picture frames and trim.


Dowel Joint
A dowel joint uses wooden dowels to connect pieces of wood.
1. Drill matching holes the same size as the dowels.
2. Apply glue to the dowels and joining surfaces.
3. Insert the dowels into one piece.
4. Fit the second piece over the projecting dowels.
5. Clamp the pieces until the glue dries.
Mortise and Tenon Joint
A mortise and tenon joint has two matching parts:
Mortise: A hole cut into one piece of wood.
Tenon: A projecting part shaped on another piece to fit into the mortise.
To make the mortise, drill to the required depth and use a sharp chisel to remove excess
wood and square the corners. Mark the tenon carefully and use a backsaw to cut it to
shape.


Mortise and Tenon Joint: Think of “tenant”—someone who dwells
inside a place—to help you remember that the tenon (the peg) goes
inside the mortise (the hole).

(d) Project with Door or Lid Fastened with
Inset Hinges


An inset hinge sits in a shallow recess, called a gain, cut into the wood so the hinge leaf fits
into the surface.
How to inset a butt hinge:
1. Put the door or lid in position.
2. Position the hinges and mark where they will sit on both pieces.
3. Mark the width and depth of each gain.
4. Score around the marked area with a chisel and wooden mallet.
5. Make shallow cuts with the grain and carefully chisel out the gain.
6. Position the hinge and insert one screw in each hinge first.
7. Check that everything fits and moves correctly before installing the remaining screws.

Explore Woodworking Training and
Apprenticeships


If you enjoy woodworking, there are several ways to keep developing your skills. Depending
on the type of work that interests you, you might learn through a technical program, an
apprenticeship, or training provided by an employer.
Technical and Woodworking Programs
High schools, technical schools, community colleges, and other postsecondary programs may
offer training in woodworking, cabinetmaking, carpentry, construction, or related fields.
Programs can provide hands-on experience with measuring and layout, joinery, cabinet
construction, shop drawings, wood properties, and modern woodworking equipment.
Carpenter and Cabinetmaker Training
The United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners of America (UBC) provides
apprenticeship and advanced training through regional training centers. Its crafts include
general carpentry as well as millworkers and cabinetmakers. Training centers offer
apprentice classes and additional skills and certification courses.

Home Depot Provides Free Woodwork Classes
If you are age 12 or younger, The Home Depot Kids Workshops are a free way to get more
hands-on experience building projects and using basic tools. A different project is offered at
participating stores on the first Saturday of each month.
Completing a small project can give you more practice following plans, assembling parts, and
seeing how individual pieces come together to make something useful or fun.
Older Scouts can explore The Home Depot's Free Livestream & On-Demand DIY Workshops
on a range of home improvement projects.
With your parent or guardian, check the schedule and register for a workshop near you.

Woodworking books may demonstrate tools or equipment that are not permitted
