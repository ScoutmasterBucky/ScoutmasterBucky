GARDENING MERIT BADGE
NOTE TO COUNSELOR
Primary Safety Concerns
Gardening activities may involve working with soil, using gardening tools, wearing appropriate protective
clothing, and handling fertilizers or pesticides. The Gardening merit badge includes safety considerations
related to insect stings and, for Scouts who choose the beekeeping option in requirement 8, working
around live honeybee hives. These activities may present risks ranging from minor injuries to severe
allergic reactions or other health effects. Always follow the current Scouting America SAFE Checklist and
the Guide to Safe Scouting when completing badge requirements.
Before Gardening Activities
Qualified Supervision
● The Gardening merit badge counselor should know the proper use of gardening tools and pesticides,
and applicable local and state USDA regulations for handling bees and beekeeping equipment.
Physical Fitness
● Counselors should be aware of youth participants who may be allergic to bee stings and to
pesticides and fungicides used in gardening.
● Before a Scout works with a beehive or visits a beekeeper (requirement 8(e)), confirm whether they
are allergic to bee stings through a visit to an allergist or family physician. A Scout who is allergic
should choose a different option under requirement 8. If a Scout brings a buddy to a beekeeper visit,
that buddy should also be checked for bee sting allergies beforehand.
Safe Area or Course
● Counselors should take reasonable measures to provide participants with a safe gardening
environment and, for the beekeeping option, well-constructed hives that pass USDA inspection.
Equipment Selection and Maintenance
● Counselors should ensure gardening tools and equipment are well maintained and that any hives
follow USDA guidelines.
Personal Safety Equipment
● Counselors should ensure all participants wear proper protective gardening clothing and equipment
—gloves, long pants, an air mask, and safety glasses—when preparing and applying chemicals.
Protective gear for beekeeping includes a hat, veil, coveralls, gloves, long sleeves, pants, and other
protective devices.
Applicable Laws


● Counselors teaching this merit badge must have experience with gardening tasks and tools. As a
rule, Scouting activities may not include activities for youth that, by law, they would not be allowed to
do in the workplace. Obey all restrictions manufacturers impose or suggest for the use of their tools
or other products, or potentially hazardous chemicals such as pesticides, fungicides, and fertilizers.
Discipline
● Counselors should take reasonable measures to ensure all participants are using gardening and
beekeeping tools and equipment properly.
COUNSELOR NOTE
Purpose
Help the Scout understand the science of growing plants—preparing soil, starting seeds and setting
out seedlings, and caring for plants through harvest or flowering—while building an appreciation for
pollinators, nutrition, and sustainable pest management. Just as important, help them build an
attitude of safety-first planning before handling tools, chemicals, or, if they choose that path, live
bees.

● Walk Scouts through basic soil preparation and the difference between starting plants from seed
versus transplanting seedlings, since requirement 2 calls for three of each, for both vegetables and
flowers.
● Tie the seed germination test (requirement 4) to real gardening decisions—a Scout who tests seeds
before planting can budget seed and bed space, and can explain likely causes of poor germination
such as seed age, planting depth, moisture, and temperature.
● Use the nutritional-value requirement (requirement 3) to connect gardening to healthy eating,
comparing root and tuber crops, vegetables that bear above ground, and fruits.
● Make pollinators concrete: Have Scouts explain how and why honeybees pollinate food crops,
discuss pressures on bee populations such as habitat loss, disease, and pesticide exposure, and talk
through what the loss of pollinators would mean for the food supply (requirement 6).
● Make pest identification hands-on (requirement 7)—have Scouts identify real pests or diseased
plants in a garden and require at least one organic control method alongside a conventional one for
each.
● For requirement 8, help the Scout choose a 90-day (or harvest-length) project that fits their
interest, space, and resources—a compost bin, a vermicompost bin, a hydroponic garden, a water


garden, preparing a honey super, or a garden of their own—and set up a simple weekly observation log
from day one.
● Encourage the visit to a county extension office, nursery, farm, or botanical garden (requirement 5)
early in the process; it often shapes what the Scout chooses to grow and connects them with a real
gardener or horticulturist.
● For requirement 9, help the Scout see gardening as both a possible career path (horticulture,
landscape design, extension work) and a lifelong hobby, not just a one-time project.
