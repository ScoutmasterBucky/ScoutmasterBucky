The Story

Archery is one of the original 57 merit badges. It appeared in the first Official Handbook for Boys in 1911, and its symbol, a bow and arrow, has been on the badge ever since.

The first Archery requirements were short and demanding. A Scout had to make his own bow and arrow that would shoot 100 feet "with fair precision," post target scores at 40 and 50 yards, and "shoot so far and fast as to have six arrows in the air at once." Until the first Archery pamphlet appeared in 1920, these requirements were found only in the Scout handbook.

For its first forty years the badge was a rarity, earned by a few hundred Scouts a year at most and ranked near the bottom of the list, about 94th of 101 badges through the 1930s. That changed after 1950. Within ten years Scouts were earning more than 5,000 a year, and by 2004 they earned nearly 50,000 Archery badges in a single year. Its best ranking came in 2007, 13th of 122 badges.

The arrow on the badge has changed direction over the years. The proper way it points in each era comes from how the badge was pictured in the pamphlets and other Scouting publications of the time. On the early square and wide-crimped badges (Types A and B) the arrow points left. The khaki Type E badges made from 1947 to 1960 point it right, and the Type F badges of the 1960s point it left again. With the fully embroidered Type G badge the design was redrawn on a red background, with the bow lying flat and the arrow crossing it on a diagonal.

Archery is primarily earned at Scout camp, where Scouts have a range and equipment to work with. That is why 2020, when most summer camps closed, cut the number earned by two-thirds. Archery remains one of the most popular badges that is not required for Eagle and has ranked 17th of all merit badges every year from 2022 through 2025.

1911 requirements

  1. Make a bow and arrow which will shoot a distance of one hundred feet with fair precision.
  2. Make a total score of 350 with 60 shots in one or two meets, using standard four-foot target at forty yards, or three-foot target at thirty yards.
  3. Make a total score of 300 with 72 arrows, using standard target at a distance of fifty yards.
  4. Shoot so far and fast as to have six arrows in the air at once.

Source: The Official Handbook for Boys (1911)

Timeline

1911 One of the 57 original merit badges in the first Official Handbook for Boys.

1914 The first two Archery merit badges are earned. None were earned in 1911, 1912 or 1913.

1920 The first Archery merit badge pamphlet (Type 1C) is printed. Until now the requirements were found only in the Scout handbook.

1947 The new khaki Type E badge points the arrow to the right; it points left again from 1961.

1952 Scouts earn 1,398 Archery badges, nearly three times the year before.

1969 The fully embroidered Type G badge brings a new design: a red background with the arrow crossing the bow on a diagonal.

1974 Earned badges jump 46% in one year, to 28,438.

2004 All-time high: 49,626 Archery merit badges earned.

2007 Archery reaches its best ranking ever: 13th of 122 merit badges.

2020 With summer camps closed by the pandemic, only 10,487 are earned, down 67%.

2021 Camps reopen and Archery climbs to 10th of all merit badges.

2026 A 2026 Scouting America edition of the pamphlet (No. 35856) appears. Scouting America is moving pamphlets to PDF, so a printed copy is not yet confirmed.

What do the patch types mean?
Type A
Square (1911 - 1933). Embroidered on tan cloth and cut into squares. The Scout folded the edges under when sewing it on.
Type B
Wide border crimped (1934 - 1935). The maker folded the edge under and pressed it round, leaving a wide cloth border. Made on depression cloth and heavyweight cloth.
Type C
Tan narrow border crimped (1936 - 1942). The same crimped edge with a narrow border of about 1/8 inch, on tan cloth.
Type D
Fine twill (1942 - 1946). Wartime badges on lighter, finer-weave cloth (fine twill and war cloth) while heavier cloth went to the war effort.
Type E
Khaki narrow border crimped (1947 - 1960). Back to heavier cloth after the war, now in khaki.
Type F
Rolled edge twill (1961 - 1968). The embroidered border is rolled over the edge instead of crimped, with a gauze backing.
Type G
Cloth back (1961 - 1971). Fully embroidered with a cloth back. By about 1969 every merit badge was made this way.
Type H
Plastic back (1972 - 2002). A plastic stiffener on the back. Collectors tell them apart by the back, which can be clear, white, or a very light blue plastic, with waffle-textured and smooth molded versions. These are sorted into variations H1 through H5.
Type I
Computer designed (1993 - 1995). Flat-edged badges with machine (computer) embroidery and a wider border. Only some badges were made this way.
Type J
Scout Stuff back (2002 - 2009). The "Scout Stuff" Supply Division logo printed on the back.
Type K
BSA 100th anniversary back (2010 - 2014). The BSA 2010 anniversary logo printed on the back.
Type L
Since 1910 back (2015 - 2025). The "Since 1910" logo printed on the back, in large and small logo versions.
Type M
Scouting America back (2025 - current). The Scouting America logo printed on the back. Wildland Fire Management and Competitive Gaming, both new in 2026, start with Type M.

Dates are approximate and can vary by badge.
